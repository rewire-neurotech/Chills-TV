Method

How your chills score is computed

Your score is a rank. You answered a short set of questionnaires. Those answers go into a model that was fitted on 2,647 people who answered the same questions and watched the same videos in a peer-reviewed study. The model returns a ranked list of videos, and your position among past visitors. Every step is on this page.

1 What we measure

Before you watched, you answered a short set of questions. They come from instruments that decades of research connect to aesthetic chills: the shivers, goosebumps, and waves of cold that some experiences trigger.

AbsorptionHow completely you get pulled into sights, sounds, and imagination.Modified Tellegen Absorption Scale (MODTAS)
Being movedHow easily you're touched, by reunions, kindness, music, farewells.Kama Muta Frequency scale (KAMF)
Personality & stateNEO-FFI personality traits plus your age and demographics on the day.Self-reported

2 The reference sample

In 2023 the research team collected the same measures from 3,259 people in Southern California; 2,647 remained after quality checks. Each watched videos drawn from a validated set of 40 chills-eliciting stimuli, then reported whether chills happened and how intense they were. The dataset, ChillsDB 2.0, is published open access, so any researcher can rerun what we do here.

3 How the model learned

Every participant becomes one row of numbers. The features are the things known before the video played: age, sex, arousal, KAMF, MODTAS, and which of the 40 stimuli they watched. The label is what actually happened, chills or no chills. A small neural network (exported to ONNX) is trained to predict that label, then used at inference time to score all 40 stimuli for your answers and rank them.

4 Why we test on strangers

A model with enough freedom can memorise the very people it was fitted on and then fail on anyone new. Researchers call that overfitting, and the standard guard against it is cross-validation: training on nine tenths of the sample and scoring on the tenth the model has never seen, rotated ten times so every participant serves once as a stranger.

5 From your answers to a video

Your answers→ Scored on each scale→ Weighed by the model→ Ranked against 40 videos→ Your match

Your "Top X%" on the hub is moving to a direct comparison against the 2,647-person research sample. Until that lands, it compares your matched video's model score against other paid ChillsTV visitors so far.

6 How well it performs

On held-out participants in the published research, the pre-video features predicted whether chills would happen with about 73.5% accuracy. A coin flip would land at 50%, so the model carries real signal, and it also leaves plenty on the table.

A higher score means chills are more likely for you, and tend to run more intense, on average. It describes a propensity measured today. Your arousal shifts by the hour, and your result can shift with it.

7 The limits

Read the score as one narrow statement: how your questionnaire answers rank against this particular model and sample. The reference group comes from one region, Southern California, at one moment in time. Chills reports are self-reported. And ~73.5% accuracy means the prediction misses roughly one time in four.
